LAS NUBES · CERRO AZUL · PANAMA

Soil, Environment and Cup Quality

A research framework for specialty coffee and Panama Green Tip Geisha in a low-elevation cloud-forest environment

Prepared for

Bob Huerbsch and Sherry Huerbsch — Las Nubes, Cerro Azul, Panama

Prepared by

Daniel Giráldez — Asesor Integral de Proyectos, Néctar Nómada

Version 1.0 · Baseline research proposal · 2026

We are not trying to manufacture altitude. We are trying to understand everything else the tree experiences — and improve the variables we can actually control.

Note on identity: this document concerns the Huerbsch family property at Las Nubes, Cerro Azul, in the Panama Canal watershed of Panamá Province. It has no relationship to any farm of a similar name in Boquete, Chiriquí.

1. Executive Summary

Las Nubes sits at roughly 800 metres above sea level in a humid, heavily forested mountain landscape. For Panama Geisha, that elevation is below the band that World Coffee Research identifies as optimal. At Panama’s latitude, WCR places Geisha in the “High” optimal-altitude class, which for farms between 5° and 15° north means above 1,300 metres [1]. This is the central agronomic fact the project has to work with, and it is not negotiable.

It is also not the end of the argument. Elevation is not itself a plant-physiological variable. It is a proxy — a convenient stand-in for cooler air temperature, a wider day–night range, particular radiation conditions and, above all, a slower rate of fruit maturation. What matters to the tree is the actual set of conditions it experiences. Las Nubes possesses several of those conditions independently of its elevation: persistent cloud and mist, high relative humidity, and 70–80% tree cover in parts of the property.

There is a well-supported body of research showing that shade improves coffee quality specifically in warm, low-elevation, sub-optimal zones, while offering little or even negative benefit at high elevation where maturation is already slow [2,3,4]. The most closely comparable published trial was conducted at 700 masl under 2,600 mm of annual rainfall in Turrialba, Costa Rica — a site strikingly similar to Las Nubes — and found that increasing shade from zero to over 80% significantly increased fruit weight and bean size [2]. That is the scientific opening this project should test.

It is equally important to state the counter-evidence plainly. A recent review of coffee terroir notes that coffee grown below 850 m with rainfall above 2,110 mm per year has been less appreciated in sensory terms, showing greater bitterness, grassy character, lower aroma and increased astringency [5]. Las Nubes sits inside both of those ranges. The project is therefore working against a documented headwind, not with one.

This paper proposes a way to resolve that tension with evidence rather than opinion. It sets out a three-to-five-year programme built on four commitments:

Measure the actual microclimate and root zone rather than inferring conditions from elevation.

Convert Bob’s existing biochar and organic-soil work from a practice into a characterised, replicated, controlled experiment with an untreated control.

Instrument the field well and outsource analytical chemistry, rather than attempting to build a soil laboratory.

Follow one connected causal chain — soil to roots to leaf nutrition to plant physiology to cherry maturation to green coffee to cup — and refuse to skip links in it.

The recommended investment is modest: roughly US$1,000–2,000 for an essential field programme, or US$2,500–5,000 for a stronger one, plus a separate annual analytical budget for accredited laboratory work. Attempting to replicate a soil chemistry laboratory on the farm would cost an order of magnitude more, would not be accredited, and would not make the results more credible.

One recommendation is more important than any other, and it appears again as the first item of the action plan: no new Green Tip Geisha research block should receive biochar, compost or fertiliser until baseline soil chemistry, baseline soil physics and a characterised biochar batch exist on paper. Amending the soil before measuring it destroys the baseline permanently, and no amount of later work recovers it.

2. Las Nubes Context

Cerro Azul is a mountainous area of Panamá Province within the Chagres National Park landscape, on the continental divide, feeding rivers on both the Pacific and Caribbean slopes; the high point of the range is Cerro Jefe at roughly 1,000 m [6]. The property sits inside the catchment that supplies the Panama Canal and the drinking water of Panama City and Colón.

Two things about this location matter scientifically rather than scenically. The first is that eastern Panama forms cloud forest at unusually low elevations. In a classic survey of Panamanian cloud forest, Myers reported that cloud forest first appears on mountains at elevations as low as 750 m in eastern Panama, compared with around 2,200 m on the higher mountains of western Panama [7]. Las Nubes at approximately 800 masl is therefore not a lowland site pretending to be a cloud forest. It sits in a landscape where genuine cloud-forest conditions are documented to occur at that elevation. The name of the village is not a marketing flourish.

The second is that the cloud-forest condition is precisely the variable that matters, and it is presently unmeasured. Cloud immersion reduces incoming radiation, lowers daytime leaf and fruit temperature, raises humidity and suppresses evaporative demand. Every one of those effects points in the same direction as elevation. None of them has yet been quantified at Las Nubes.

3. Why Soil Matters — and What It Can and Cannot Do

It is tempting to treat soil as the lever that compensates for elevation. It is not, and the paper is careful not to make that claim. Soil does something narrower and more useful: it determines whether the tree is able to express whatever potential its genetics and its climate allow.

The mechanism runs through three separate systems that are often collapsed into one.

Soil chemistry decides what is available

In acidic tropical soils, the limiting factor is frequently not the absence of nutrients but their unavailability, combined with the active presence of aluminium. Aluminium becomes increasingly soluble as pH falls below about 5.5 and toxic effects become severe below about 5.0 [11]. The classic symptom in sensitive species is inhibition of root elongation. Work on coffee in Ultisols and Oxisols found that liming at rates equivalent to the exchangeable aluminium present reduced aluminium saturation of the effective CEC to below 30%, and that below pH 5.4 the buffering capacity of these soils was governed primarily by exchangeable aluminium [12]. [P]

This is why a bare pH number is insufficient. Two soils at pH 4.9 can behave completely differently depending on their exchangeable aluminium, their effective CEC and their base saturation. Those three numbers, not pH alone, describe the root environment.

Soil physics decides whether roots can reach it

A soil can be chemically adequate and still fail if roots cannot occupy it. In a humid, clay-heavy site this is the more probable constraint. Root elongation is typically halved in soils with penetration resistance above roughly 0.8–2 MPa [13], and limiting bulk density values for clay-textured soils fall in the region of 1.47–1.58 g cm⁻³ [14]. Separately, aeration becomes limiting below roughly 10% air-filled porosity, with some authors arguing 12–15% is a safer working limit [15]. [P]

Soil biology mediates both

Organic matter, microbial activity and mycorrhizal association affect nutrient cycling, aggregate stability and root health. This is the least tractable of the three at farm scale and should be measured selectively rather than continuously.

Soil does not create cup quality. It removes the limitations that prevent a tree from expressing the quality its genetics and climate would otherwise permit. That is a smaller claim, and a defensible one.

4. Green Tip Geisha and the Lower-Elevation Question

4.1 What the authoritative source actually says

World Coffee Research records Panama Geisha (accession T2722) as a tall plant of Ethiopian landrace origin, with average bean size, low yield potential, medium nutrition requirement, first production in year four, average ripening time and average cherry-to-green-bean outturn, planted at 3,000–4,000 plants per hectare under single-stem pruning [1]. Its cup quality potential at high altitude is classified as exceptional — the highest category in the catalogue [1]. [P]

Three disease and pest ratings matter for a humid, biodiverse site. WCR rates Panama Geisha as having intermediate resistance to coffee leaf rust, and as susceptible to both nematodes and coffee berry disease [1]. Coffee berry disease is not currently present in Central America, but nematode susceptibility is immediately relevant, and it is the reason nematode analysis appears in the baseline programme rather than being deferred. [P]

The optimal altitude rating is the one that governs this project. WCR classifies the variety as “High,” and for farms at latitudes between 5° and 15° north or south — which includes all of Panama — “High” is defined as above 1,300 m [1]. Las Nubes at ~800 masl is roughly 500 m below that threshold. [P]

4.2 On the term &quot;Green Tip&quot;

The WCR entry for Panama Geisha records leaf tip colour as &quot;Green or Bronze&quot; within the same variety [1]. In other words, the authoritative varietal catalogue treats both leaf-tip phenotypes as falling under one genetic entity, and WCR’s genetic diversity analyses describe Panamanian Geisha descended from T2722 as distinct and uniform [1]. [P]

Panamanian producers commonly distinguish Green Tip from Bronze Tip and associate them with different sensory tendencies. That distinction may well be real and useful in practice. But this paper will not describe Green Tip as a separate cultivar, and neither should any Las Nubes technical or commercial document, unless and until genetic evidence specific to that separation is published. The correct wording is: a leaf-tip phenotype or selection within Panama Geisha. [F/P]

Practical consequence for the trial: the planting material’s origin, seed lot, nursery and supplier must be recorded, because &quot;Green Tip Geisha&quot; is not by itself a sufficient description of what was planted.

4.3 The mechanism behind the altitude effect

Altitude acts on quality largely through temperature and its effect on the rate of fruit development. Lower temperature prolongs the bean-filling period, giving more time for the accumulation of the constituents that later become flavour precursors [4]. Shade produces a directionally similar effect: canopy shade has been reported to lower temperature by around 2–4 °C relative to full sun [4]. [P]

Critically, the benefit is conditional. In sub-optimal, warm, low-elevation environments, reduction of temperature by shade is positive. In cool high-elevation environments, where maturation is already slow, the additional cooling is offset by the loss of radiation, and shade effects on quality can be neutral or negative [3,4]. [P]

Muschler’s trial in a sub-optimal zone of Costa Rica is the closest published analogue to Las Nubes: 700 masl, 2,600 mm annual rainfall. Increasing shade from 0% to more than 80% significantly increased fruit weight and bean size; the proportion of large beans (&gt;6.7 mm) rose from 49% to 69% in Caturra and from 43% to 72% in Catimor, while the conversion percentage from fresh fruit to export green coffee was unaffected by treatment [2]. [P]

4.4 The honest counterweight

Two findings must be held alongside the optimistic ones.

First, the Williams et al. review of coffee terroir reports that coffee grown at low altitude (below 850 m) with high rainfall (above 2,110 mm per year) has been less appreciated, showing more substantial bitterness, grassy flavour, lower aroma and increased astringency [5]. Las Nubes falls inside both parameters. [P]

Second, shade at Las Nubes is already very high — 70–80% cover in parts of the property. The published benefit of shade is a benefit of moving from insufficient shade to substantial shade. It does not follow that more shade is better without limit, and over-shading is a recognised risk, including through reduced carbon assimilation and altered pest and disease pressure. It is entirely possible that the correct intervention at Las Nubes is selective canopy management to increase light in some blocks, not additional shade. [H]

4.5 The research question, stated precisely

Las Nubes cannot change its elevation. Elevation is partly a proxy for temperature, radiation and maturation rate. The question is whether a cool, shaded, cloud-immersed, biodiverse environment, combined with deliberate root-zone and soil management, allows Panama Green Tip Geisha to express exceptional quality at this site — and if so, under what specific microclimatic and edaphic conditions.

We are testing complementarity and limitation-removal. We are not testing substitution. The paper does not claim, and the project should never claim, that shade, biochar or soil fertility replace altitude.

A note of encouragement grounded in evidence: a 2025 study of Panama Geisha from four production zones found that sensory attributes differed significantly across Boquete, Renacimiento and Tierras Altas, while the main physicochemical parameters did not distinguish the zones [16]. Two things follow. Panamanian Geisha does express measurable regional identity — which is the premise of the whole exercise. And the instrument panel did not detect what the sensory panel detected, which is a strong argument for investing in disciplined sensory evaluation rather than in analytical chemistry of the green bean. [P]

5. Soil Chemistry Framework

The purpose of the baseline is to describe the root environment by block and by depth, once, properly. Everything afterwards is measured against it.

5.1 A caution about &quot;ideal values&quot;

This paper deliberately does not supply a table of target values for every parameter. There are two reasons. First, interpretation depends on the extraction method: a phosphorus figure obtained with modified Olsen is not comparable to one obtained with Mehlich-3, and an inter-laboratory comparison programme in Costa Rica found variation between laboratories of roughly 10–20% for soil determinations and 10–15% for foliar determinations even under a coordinated scheme [17]. Second, coffee-specific critical values are region-derived. Always record the extractant and method alongside the number, and always use the same laboratory and method for repeat sampling — a change of laboratory mid-programme will manufacture a false trend. [P]

6. Soil Physics and Drainage

If the paper has one non-obvious argument, it is this: at a humid, clay-heavy, high-rainfall site, root-zone oxygen is likely to be at least as important as nutrition, and it is far less often measured.

6.1 High soil moisture is not the same thing as waterlogging

These are distinct conditions and conflating them leads to the wrong intervention.

High soil moisture means water is abundant and available. In a well-structured soil with adequate large pores, roots can be surrounded by plentiful water and still have access to oxygen, because air occupies the larger pores while water is held in the smaller ones. This is a favourable condition.

Poor root aeration means the air-filled pore space has fallen far enough that oxygen diffusion to the root surface becomes limiting. The commonly used critical threshold is around 10% air-filled porosity, with 12–15% suggested as a safer limit [15]. Below that, root elongation slows sharply, root respiration is impaired, and nutrient uptake — which is an energy-requiring process — degrades even when nutrients are abundant. Compacted soils are also more susceptible to waterlogging after heavy rain [13]. [P]

The practical distinction: a soil that is wet and well-structured is an asset at Las Nubes. A soil that is wet and compacted, or wet over an impeding clay layer, is a hidden yield and quality ceiling that no fertiliser programme will lift. The diagnostic difference is visible in a soil pit — grey or mottled colours, orange-red concentrations along old root channels, and a sour smell indicate periodic anaerobic conditions. [H/?]

7. Leaf-Tissue Analysis

Soil testing indicates what is present or extractable. Foliar analysis indicates what the coffee tree actually absorbed.

This distinction is the reason both are necessary. A soil can contain abundant potassium that the tree cannot take up because of competition from calcium, because roots cannot reach it, or because waterlogging has impaired uptake energetics. Only the leaf reveals that.

Foliar analysis at Las Nubes should cover N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, B and Cu.

7.1 Standardised sampling is what makes the numbers comparable

Leaf nutrient concentration varies systematically with leaf position, leaf age, cultivar, phenological stage and season. If sampling is not standardised, year-to-year comparison is meaningless and treatment differences will be swamped by sampling noise. Extension guidance for coffee is explicit that tissue analysis must follow standard procedures and be interpreted against sufficiency ranges tied to a defined plant part and growth stage [20]. [P]

The convention most commonly encountered in coffee practice is to sample a defined leaf pair — usually the third or fourth counting down from the tip — on an actively growing primary branch of a bearing tree, pooling around a hundred leaves across several dozen trees within one block, with pre-flowering used as the standard reference timing. This paper does not attach a citation to those specific numbers, because protocols differ between institutions and the sufficiency ranges a laboratory reports are only valid for the protocol that generated them. Fix the exact leaf position, sample size and timing in writing with the chosen laboratory before the first sample is taken, and then never change it. [T]

For this project, standardise and then record on every sample: leaf pair position; branch position in the canopy (upper, middle, lower third); tree age and cultivar; phenological stage at sampling; date and time; block and treatment; and whether the sampled branch was bearing fruit. Sample all treatments on the same day. Use paper envelopes, not plastic bags.

7.2 On sufficiency ranges

Published sufficiency ranges exist and are useful as orientation. One Brazilian study, working from paired soil and leaf data, estimated diagnostic-leaf sufficiency ranges for coffee of approximately 33.4–35.8 g kg⁻¹ N, 1.4–1.6 g kg⁻¹ P, 24.4–27.0 g kg⁻¹ K, 11.9–13.6 g kg⁻¹ Ca, 3.8–4.5 g kg⁻¹ Mg and 1.4–1.8 g kg⁻¹ S [21]. [P]

Two cautions. These values are derived under Brazilian conditions with Brazilian analytical methods; they are a starting reference, not a target for Las Nubes. And the sampling protocol used to generate a range must match the protocol used to collect the sample being compared to it. Over time, the most valuable interpretive dataset will be Las Nubes’ own multi-year record of leaf composition against its own yield and cup outcomes.

Note that WCR classifies Panama Geisha’s nutrition requirement as Medium — neither low-input nor demanding [1]. This is an argument against aggressive fertilisation and in favour of correcting specific limitations identified by analysis. [P]

8. Microclimate Monitoring

Actual microclimate is more useful than assuming conditions from elevation alone. This is the intellectual core of the Las Nubes case, and it only becomes an argument once there is data behind it.

If Las Nubes intends to argue that its environment behaves more favourably than its elevation implies, it must be able to show measured air temperature, measured day–night range, measured humidity, measured cloud immersion and measured light. Without that record the claim is a story. With two years of it, the claim becomes a dataset that can be placed alongside data from higher-elevation sites.

9. Bob’s Biochar Program

The purpose of Bob’s biochar experiment is not to prove that biochar creates a better cup. It is to measure whether it changes the soil, whether the tree responds, whether the cherry responds, and only then whether the cup responds.

9.1 What the evidence actually supports

The evidence for biochar in tropical acidic soils is genuinely encouraging, genuinely conditional, and genuinely thin where coffee cup quality is concerned. All three of those need to be said.

The encouraging part: the largest global meta-analysis found that biochar produced approximately a 25% average yield increase in the tropics at a median application rate of 15 t ha⁻¹, while having essentially no effect — around −3% — in temperate latitudes. The mechanism identified was liming and fertilisation, consistent with the low pH, low fertility and low fertiliser inputs typical of arable tropical soils, and high-nutrient biochars stimulated yield substantially more than low-nutrient ones [22]. Las Nubes is exactly the soil context in which biochar has the best published record. [P]

The conditional part: a meta-analysis focused specifically on acidic soils found that in tropical climates biochar typically increased porosity by 23%, mean weight diameter of aggregates by 46% and yield by 38%, while decreasing bulk density by 19% — but that the minimum effect was observed in soils below pH 5 [23]. If the Las Nubes baseline returns a pH below 5, expectations should be revised downward, not upward. Similarly, high soil CEC attenuates the liming effect of biochar, and biochar produced at moderate pyrolysis temperatures has a greater liming effect than material produced at very low (≤300 °C) or very high (&gt;700 °C) temperatures [18]. [P]

The thin part: the International Biochar Initiative’s own coffee review stated that there was still insufficient biochar trial data to predict impacts on coffee yield or tree growth rates [24]. A field study applying coffee-pulp biochar in a Colombian coffee system reported slight and promising improvements — modest increases in nutrient availability, soil pH and arbuscular mycorrhizal colonisation of roots [25]. &quot;Slight and promising&quot; is the honest current state of the coffee-specific literature. A trial in Vietnam using coffee-husk biochar in acidic soils under Robusta and black pepper additionally observed reductions in plant-parasitic nematode and Phytophthora populations, while concluding that more research is required [26] — a finding worth noting given Geisha’s documented nematode susceptibility [1]. [P]

No credible source claims biochar improves cup quality. The project should not imply one exists. [H]

9.2 Biochar is not one material

This is the point that most often defeats farm biochar programmes. A comprehensive meta-data review drawing on roughly 5,400 papers and over 50,800 data points concluded that pyrolysis type (fast or slow) plays only a minor role, that higher pyrolysis temperature raises carbon content and specific surface area and increases longevity — with temperatures above 500 °C generally giving half-lives beyond 1,000 years — but that feedstock selection has the largest single influence on final biochar properties [27]. [P]

Feedstock class matters in a way that is directly relevant to Bob’s materials. Biochars produced from animal litter and solid-waste feedstocks show lower surface area, lower carbon content and lower volatile matter but higher CEC than biochars from crop residue and wood biomass, even at higher pyrolysis temperatures, because of differences in lignin, cellulose and moisture content [28]. A poultry-litter char and an oak char are not interchangeable inputs. [P]

There is also a safety dimension that a farm-scale programme should not ignore. A study characterising 24 biochars from eight feedstocks — including chicken manure — across 450, 600 and 750 °C found a significant increase in the sum of the 16 US EPA polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at 750 °C compared with material produced in the 450–600 °C range, while some potentially toxic elements (Zn, Cu, Cr) decreased above 600 °C [29]. Very high production temperatures are not automatically better. Documenting the thermal regime is a food-safety measure as well as an agronomic one. [P]

10. Experimental Design

10.1 Two preconditions that cannot be waived

No application dose is assigned in this document, and none should be assigned until both of the following exist:

Baseline soil analysis is complete for every block that will carry a treatment — because the dose of an alkaline amendment must be matched to the exchangeable acidity and exchangeable aluminium actually present, and because a post-amendment soil test cannot be retro-fitted into a baseline.

The biochar itself has been characterised — because &quot;10 tonnes of biochar per hectare&quot; describes a volume, not a treatment. Without pH, ash, carbon, nutrient content and particle size, the experiment cannot be interpreted and cannot be repeated.

Applying material before these two conditions are met does not merely delay the science; it forecloses it.

10.3 Why the untreated control is non-negotiable

Coffee yield swings substantially between years for reasons entirely unrelated to soil management: biennial bearing, rainfall distribution, flowering events, pest pressure and tree age. A treated block that yields better in 2029 than in 2028 tells us nothing at all. A treated block that yields better than an adjacent untreated control in the same year, under the same weather, on the same soil, with the same trees, tells us something.

The same logic applies with even more force to cup quality, where season-to-season variation in a single lot can easily exceed the difference any soil treatment would produce. Every sensory comparison in this programme must include a control lot harvested and processed the same way in the same season.

10.4 Replication, randomisation and pseudoreplication

The recommended layout is a randomised complete block design. Divide the trial area into blocks that each capture a similar range of slope, aspect, shade and soil, then randomly assign all five treatments within each block. Three to four blocks gives three to four true replicates per treatment.

The failure to avoid is pseudoreplication. Applying T1 to one contiguous area and then treating the twenty trees inside it as twenty replicates is not replication — those trees share one soil patch, one drainage line and one canopy. They are twenty measurements of one experimental unit. The statistical unit is the plot, not the tree. Report the plot mean.

Practical guidance for the layout: use plots of at least 9–12 trees and analyse only the inner trees, leaving the outer row as a guard row so that amendments and root systems from adjacent treatments do not contaminate the measured trees. Record tree IDs permanently — aluminium tags, not paint.

Where the trial involves the new Green Tip Geisha planting, remember that WCR records first production in year four [1]. The design must therefore anticipate a long vegetative phase in which growth, survival, leaf nutrition and root health are the measured outcomes, and cup quality is simply not yet available. That is expected, not a failure of the programme. [P]

11.1 A warning about consumer NPK probes

Inexpensive multi-function probes marketed as measuring soil &quot;NPK&quot; alongside pH and moisture should not be used as analytical instruments in this programme. They do not perform chemical extraction, and nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium availability in soil cannot be inferred from bulk electrical properties. Data from such devices will not survive scrutiny and, worse, may lead to fertiliser decisions that are simply wrong.

Spend the equivalent money on one properly calibrated pH/EC meter, a good balance and a reliable data logger. Three trustworthy instruments beat ten unreliable ones, and this project’s credibility with Bob, Sherry and any future partner rests entirely on whether its numbers hold up.

13. Recommended Equipment and Budget

13.1 Why not build a laboratory

It would be technically possible to spend US$10,000–20,000 assembling soil chemistry capability at Las Nubes. It would be a mistake, for four reasons.

The capital cost buys equipment, not capability. Reliable CEC, exchangeable aluminium and available phosphorus determinations require reagent management, controlled storage, method validation and analyst time — recurring costs that dwarf the instrument price.

The output would not be accredited. Analab’s value comes substantially from ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation [33]. An unaccredited farm result carries less weight with a buyer, a partner or a journal than a modestly priced accredited one.

The variables that justify capital spending are the dynamic ones. Soil chemistry changes slowly. Microclimate changes hourly. Money spent on loggers generates far more information per dollar than money spent on glassware.

It would consume the project’s scarcest resource, which is attention. Running a laboratory is a job. Running the trial is also a job.

The recommended model is: field instrumentation + rigorous sampling + periodic accredited laboratory confirmation.

14. Sampling Schedule

The organising principle is straightforward: measure fast-changing variables often and slow-changing variables rarely. Soil chemistry does not need to be measured monthly. Measuring it monthly wastes money and generates noise that will be mistaken for signal, because the analytical variability between rounds will exceed the real change.

14.1 Recommended design

Four meaningful research blocks, each capturing a distinct combination of slope, aspect, shade and apparent soil condition.

Three soil depths initially — approximately 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm and 40–60 cm. The deeper horizons are where aluminium and compaction problems hide, and where surface-applied amendments do not reach.

Composite samples where scientifically appropriate: 10–15 sub-samples taken across a plot at the same depth, mixed thoroughly, and one representative sub-sample submitted. Composite within a plot and a depth; never composite across treatments or depths.

One soil pit per block in Year 0, described and photographed in full. This is the only way to see structure, mottling, rooting depth and impeding layers.

Permanent, tagged sampling points so that later rounds return to the same locations.

15.1 Green coffee and sensory protocol

For every experimental lot, record moisture, water activity, bean density, screen size, physical defects and outturn. The accepted moisture range for green coffee is 10–12% wet basis [31], and lots outside it should be flagged before cupping because the difference will show in the cup and will be mistaken for a treatment effect.

Use a single standardised processing method for all treatments in the early years. A clean washed process is the right choice: it is the most repeatable, the least likely to mask agronomic differences, and it is the process used in the Panama Geisha regional study that successfully detected zone differences [16]. Fermentation and experimental processing are legitimate future work — but introducing them now would confound the agronomic question the trial exists to answer.

Sensory evaluation must be blind, randomised, replicated and standardised in roast and brew. Record both scores and descriptors. Descriptors of potential interest include floral, jasmine, citrus, bergamot, stone fruit, tropical fruit, sweetness, acidity quality and finish — but the panel must not be told to look for them. Priming a panel to find jasmine will produce jasmine. These are outcomes to be observed, not targets to be forced. A standardised lexicon and a fixed roast level and brew method are prerequisites for any terroir assessment [5].

Year 2

Nutrition, physiology, flowering and fruit development.

Second full soil chemistry round. Foliar analysis twice. SPAD tracking. Flowering dates, fruit set, crop load recorded by plot. Days flowering-to-ripe calculated for the first time. First cherry Brix and weight data on bearing trees. Interim technical report. DECISION GATE: continue, modify or discontinue treatments.

Years 3–4

Harvest separation, standardised processing, sensory evaluation.

Harvest by plot, kept strictly separate. Standardised washed processing for all treatments. Full green coffee characterisation. Blind, randomised, replicated cupping with control lots in every session. First treatment-versus-control cup comparison. Second nematode round. DECISION GATE: is there a measurable, replicated treatment effect at any point in the chain?

Years 4–5+

Repeatability across seasons and refinement.

Repeat harvest and sensory evaluation across at least two additional seasons. Test whether effects are consistent or season-dependent. Refine the best-performing treatment and consider a second-generation trial (dose response, or extension to additional blocks). Publish or present. Only at this stage does the project have grounds to make any claim about Las Nubes terroir.

17. Risks and Limitations

Table 17 — Principal risks

Risk

Why it is real

Mitigation

The elevation constraint is not overcome

WCR places optimal Geisha altitude at this latitude above 1,300 m [1], and low-altitude high-rainfall coffee has documented sensory disadvantages [5]. Las Nubes is 500 m below and inside both risk ranges.

Frame the project as characterising the site honestly rather than proving a thesis. A well-documented negative result protects the family from a much more expensive mistake later. Survey the higher ground on the property.

Over-shading

The shade benefit documented at low elevation [2] is a benefit of adding shade to insufficient shade. At 70–80% cover, Las Nubes may be past the optimum, and excessive shading reduces carbon assimilation.

Measure PAR against an open-ground reference. Treat selective canopy thinning as a legitimate candidate intervention, and if trialled, treat it as a proper treatment with a control.

Waterlogging and poor root aeration

Humid site, clay-heavy soils, high rainfall, sloping terrain. Aeration below ~10% air-filled porosity limits root function [15].

Soil pits in Year 0. Continuous soil moisture logging. Look explicitly for mottling and impeding layers. Drainage may prove more valuable than any amendment.

Aluminium toxicity in acid soils

Al solubility rises sharply below pH 5.5 [11]; Catuaí is reported as Al-sensitive [10]; Geisha’s tolerance at this site is unknown.

Measure exchangeable Al and Al saturation, not just pH. Consider a documented liming treatment with its own control after baseline.

Nematode pressure

WCR classifies Panama Geisha as susceptible to nematodes [1], and the site is warm, humid and biodiverse.

Nematode analysis in the Year 0 baseline, before planting where possible. Repeat every two years.

Biochar effect below expectations

Minimum biochar effect on acidic soil properties and yield was observed at pH below 5 [23]; high soil CEC attenuates the liming effect [18]; coffee-specific trial data remains insufficient to predict yield impacts [24].

Set expectations from the baseline pH and CEC, not from the headline tropical average. Keep T3 (organic amendment without biochar) in the design so the char’s specific contribution is testable.

Confounding by processing

Post-harvest processing can easily produce larger sensory differences than any agronomic treatment.

One standardised washed process for all treatments during the comparison years. No experimental fermentation in the trial lots.

Pseudoreplication and under-powered design

The most common failure mode in on-farm research. Produces confident conclusions from data that cannot support them.

Randomised complete blocks, three to four true replicates, guard rows, plot-level analysis. Accept that some questions will remain unresolved.

Data discontinuity

Logger failure, changing laboratories, staff turnover, unlabelled samples.

Monthly data download and off-site backup. Same laboratory and same method throughout. Written protocols so the work survives a change of personnel.

Over-claiming

Commercial pressure to describe hypotheses as findings.

The evidence labels in this document. No sensory or agronomic claim leaves the farm until it has been replicated across at least two seasons.

One further limitation deserves to be named directly. Even a well-executed programme may end with the finding that soil management at Las Nubes produced measurable changes in soil chemistry and physics, measurable changes in leaf nutrition, and no detectable change in the cup. That would not be a failed project. It would be a real and useful answer, and it is roughly what the current state of the coffee-specific biochar literature would predict [24,25].

18. Decision Criteria

Research programmes drift when they lack pre-agreed criteria for continuing, changing or stopping. The following gates should be agreed by Bob, Sherry and Daniel before Year 0 begins, so that later decisions are made against a standard set in advance rather than against the mood of the moment.

Full soil chemistry for all treatment blocks, soil pits described, biochar batch characterised, and the biochar protocol written. If any one is missing, do not apply. No exceptions.

A detectable, directionally consistent difference between treated plots and T0 in at least one of: pH, exchangeable Al, Al saturation, bulk density, penetration resistance, infiltration. If none, reconsider dose or discontinue the weakest treatments.

A difference between treated plots and T0 in foliar nutrient concentration, SPAD, growth or root score. Soil change without plant response means the amendment is not reaching the tree.

A difference in cherry Brix, cherry weight, ripening uniformity or crop load. If soil and leaf changed but the cherry did not, the chain is broken and the cup is unlikely to differ.

Survival, vigour, absence of severe nematode or rust damage, and evidence of adequate maturation. If these fail, the varietal question is answered independently of the soil question — and answering it early is worth more than any amendment result.

19. Conclusions

Las Nubes at ~800 masl sits well below the elevation band that World Coffee Research identifies as optimal for Panama Geisha at this latitude [1]. This is the governing constraint and should be stated plainly in every document the project produces.

The site nonetheless possesses genuine, documented cloud-forest character. Cloud forest is recorded in eastern Panama at elevations as low as 750 m [7], which means the environment is not merely being described charitably. But that character has never been measured at Las Nubes, and until it is, it remains a description rather than an argument.

The published evidence supports a real, conditional opening: shade improves coffee quality in warm, low-elevation, sub-optimal zones [2,4], with the closest analogue trial conducted at 700 masl under 2,600 mm rainfall [2]. It equally supports a real warning: low-altitude, high-rainfall coffee has documented sensory disadvantages [5], and Las Nubes falls inside both parameters. Both must be carried forward.

In a humid, clay-heavy site, root-zone aeration is likely to matter as much as nutrition and is far less often measured. High soil moisture and waterlogging are not the same condition and require opposite responses. Soil pits in Year 0 are the highest-value, lowest-cost activity in the entire programme.

Exchangeable aluminium and aluminium saturation — not pH alone — describe the root chemical environment in acid tropical soils [11,12]. Given that the existing planting is Catuaí, reported as aluminium-sensitive [10], these are priority measurements.

Biochar has the strongest published record precisely in low-pH, low-fertility tropical soils, through liming and fertilisation [22], but its effect is smallest below pH 5 [23], depends more on feedstock than on any other production variable [27], and remains insufficiently studied in coffee to predict outcomes [24]. Bob’s work should be characterised and tested, not assumed.

Soil testing shows what is present; foliar analysis shows what the tree absorbed. Both are necessary, and neither is interpretable without standardised sampling.

The correct investment model is field instrumentation plus rigorous sampling plus periodic accredited laboratory confirmation. Building a soil chemistry laboratory would cost far more, deliver unaccredited results, and consume the attention the trial needs.

The programme must run three to five years minimum. Panama Geisha does not produce until year four [1], and a single harvest cannot establish a terroir effect.

The most important single action is also the simplest: measure the soil and characterise the biochar before anything is applied to any new Green Tip Geisha block. This is irreversible in a way that no other decision in the programme is.

We are not trying to manufacture altitude. We are trying to understand everything else the tree experiences — and improve the variables we can actually control.
